Front page Housing · Permitted development · Health · England

“Substandard” by deregulation, not by conversion: a UCL Bartlett report asks the government to end office-to-home permitted development

The Architects’ Journal (28 October) reports a 217-page study from the UCL Bartlett School of Planning that calls for the repeal of the permitted development rights (PDR) allowing offices and other non-residential buildings to become homes without full planning permission. Its lead author, Ben Clifford, says the planning deregulation “has in turn led to a large quantity of substandard housing”.

According to the AJ, a survey for the report found that 76.5% of people living in converted properties reported at least one housing quality issue, and 40% of the homes are close to potential sources of noise and air pollution. Of the 42 PDR homes the researchers monitored, all but two recorded air pollution and carbon dioxide levels above World Health Organization limits. Separate modelling by the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, covering small office-to-residential flats (around 30% of PDR housing), estimates 28.8 excess deaths and 100.8 asthma cases per 10,000 residents; that is a model, not a count. The UCL team also found signs of depression in 22% of residents of PDR homes against a national average of 16%, and said the poorest-quality homes are often concentrated in more deprived areas.

The history matters to the argument. PDR was introduced in 2013 for offices and widened two years later to shops, agricultural buildings, industrial estates and business parks. The authors stress that the problems “stemmed from the removal of important planning process regulations, rather than being inherent to building conversions”, and the report adds a design brief for architects working on conversions: optimise the placement and size of windows, make cross-ventilation a priority, and test year-round thermal performance, moisture and air quality together. The AJ says a 39-strong coalition made a similar call last year and that the housing ministry (MHCLG) had been contacted for comment; this paper found no government reply and has read the AJ’s report of the study, not the 217 pages themselves.

CRIT · Question of the day

If a change of use needs no planning permission, who answers for the window that was never added? And if the fault lies in the process rather than the conversion, what should an architect’s drawing guarantee that the process no longer does?

  1. Draw one small single-aspect office conversion flat in plan and section. Mark the only route air can take through it, then redraw it with a second opening that makes cross-ventilation possible.
  2. List the study’s problems (air, CO₂, noise, space, light, overheating) in two columns: fixable by design, fixable only by regulation. Say which column your own Diploma project quietly depends on.

London · Profession · Procurement (29 Oct)

London relaunches its architecture framework after small practices said it brought them no work

According to the Architects’ Journal (29 October), City Hall launched on Tuesday 27 October the fourth version of the GLA’s Architecture and Urbanism (A+U) framework, advertised at an estimated £45m of work. The current framework began in March 2023 and ends next year; the new one will run from 1 January 2028 to 31 December 2031, with nine lots (from strategic planning to housing, public realm and small projects), at most three lots per firm, an unlimited number of suppliers and applications open until 8 December. The GLA says it was redesigned after “extensive stakeholder feedback”, with guidance and support for small organisations, a faster procurement process and new assessment criteria to broaden participation.

The numbers behind the redesign are the point. The AJ says the previous framework enabled £15m of services over its last three years, about a third of what its predecessor commissioned (£41m over four years), and that practices including McCloy + Muchemwa and Nimtim won no work at all despite a framework set up to promote small and diverse firms. Others did well: East calls the Oxford Street pedestrianisation “dream commission” its highlight, and Archio praises the dedicated small-sites lot. Pitman Tozer’s Luke Tozer, who plans to apply, hopes social value scoring “avoids being another tick-box procurement exercise”. The piece is built on the GLA’s statement and a handful of practices; it reports no independent measure of how the new rules will change who wins.

CRITA framework that admits everyone but commissions few is a list, not an opportunity. Imagine you are a three-person practice: which of the nine lots would you bid for, and what could you show a council in two pages that a drawing portfolio cannot?

Italy · Milan · Stadium · Heritage (29 Oct)

Foster + Partners and Manica unveil the stadium that will replace San Siro, while Inter fans unfurl a banner against it

The Guardian and Dezeen (both 29 October) report that Inter and Milan have revealed the Generali Stadium, 71,500 seats on the site of the existing San Siro, which the Guardian says is expected to cost the clubs €1.2bn; works are due to start in 2028 for the 2031–32 season. Dezeen describes a pre-cast concrete facade of X-framed supports about 60 m tall carrying the bowl and a double-curved roof, naturally ventilated upper loggias, 27,500 m² of photovoltaic panels, an all-electric operation with a LEED Gold target, and a 281,000 m² masterplan with public space. Norman Foster says the form is “low in the body, and rises up at either end”; David Manica says the seats are brought as close to the pitch as possible because “San Siro is a feeling”.

What is being replaced is the argument. Both outlets say the Stacchini-designed stadium will be demolished once the new one is complete (the Guardian dates its opening to 1926, Dezeen to 1925). Dezeen notes that an earlier Populous scheme was blocked in 2023 by Lombardy’s heritage commission because of the existing building’s cultural significance, and that the clubs bought the stadium after the city council approved the sale in October 2025; the Guardian says they paid €197m for it and its parking areas, and that a group of Inter fans interrupted the gala with a banner reading “you will never extinguish the lights at San Siro”. Dezeen reports no criticism of the design itself, and the cost figure is the Guardian’s, not the architects’.

CRITWhen a building is defended as “a feeling”, what must its replacement carry across: the proximity of the seats, the noise, or the structure? Draw the old and new bowls at one scale, shade what will be demolished, and name one thing in the new bowl that could not be built without the old.

Profession · AI · Opinion (29 Oct)

“Maybe we have defined the profession too narrowly”: Piers Taylor answers AI by asking architects to host rather than author

In a Dezeen opinion piece (29 October), the architect and educator Piers Taylor argues that the profession’s instinct to defend its territory against AI is “absolutely the wrong response”. He says architecture has tied its value to drawings, images and authored objects, and that the pay model behind it (low fees, long hours, unpaid competitions in exchange for recognition) would only be pushed lower if AI makes production faster and cheaper. “If AI can do most of what we think an architect does,” runs the headline, “maybe we have defined the profession too narrowly.”

His alternative is that architects are most useful in unresolved situations, where they diagnose problems, connect kinds of knowledge and make spatial judgements, acting as “less author and more host”. He would have clients pay for briefing, bringing expertise together, full-scale testing, post-occupancy review and long-term stewardship, work he says is now unpaid or undervalued, and he cites his own Studio in the Woods, which has run for 20 years, as a small model. The piece contains no data on fees or on AI’s effect on them; it is one practitioner’s argument, and this paper read it through a page summary rather than the full text.

CRITIf a machine can draft the drawings, what is left in your portfolio that a client would pay for? Name three services an architect could be paid for that produce no drawing, and show which of them your own project already performs.

Finland · Muhos · Datacentres · Land (29 Oct)

Finnish police open a criminal investigation after a Google subsidiary cleared forest for a datacentre before the impact assessment was complete

The Guardian (29 October) reports that Finnish police are investigating possible breaches of environmental law at the planned Google datacentre in Muhos, in northern Finland, where authorities allege that Tuike Finland, a Google subsidiary, cleared and drained 330 hectares of forest before the required environmental impact assessments were finished. Police cite “ambiguities and shortcomings in the drainage and water management”. Earlier this month the Supervisory Agency ordered work to stop at Muhos and at Otanmäki, about 100 km to the south-east; campaigners estimate that 250,000 trees were felled at Muhos and 150,000 at Otanmäki. Google’s Finland manager has apologised, saying the company acted on “the best understanding we had of the legislation at the time”.

The scale is what makes this an infrastructure story. Finnish authorities are considering applications for 145 new datacentres, including a €13bn AI project that Google calls its largest investment in Europe, and the prime minister, Petteri Orpo, called attracting investment a “top priority” when it was announced in September. More than 93,000 people have signed a petition for stricter regulation and compensation, and a University of Helsinki professor of computer science, Jussi Kangasharju, is quoted saying “for Finland, there are no benefits”; that is one expert’s view, and the Guardian does not report Google’s response to the police investigation itself.

CRITA datacentre is drawn as a box and a power line, yet this one began as 330 hectares of forest and a drainage plan. Draw its first year as a section through ground, water and roof, and mark which operations a permit should have allowed only after an assessment.

서양 · 플라톤 『국가』: 큰 글씨로 읽는 영혼

시대적 배경

기원전 404년 아테네는 펠로폰네소스 전쟁에서 패했고, 곧 30인 참주 정권이 들어섰다. 민주정이 회복된 뒤인 기원전 399년에는 소크라테스가 재판을 받고 사형당했다. 플라톤은 이 사건들을 겪은 세대이고, 『국가』는 보통 기원전 380~370년대에 쓴 것으로 추정한다. 책은 소크라테스가 피레우스 항구의 케팔로스 집에서 있었던 대화를 전하는 형식이다. 대화의 첫 질문은 소박하다. 정의로운 사람은 정말 불의한 사람보다 잘 사는가?

핵심 사상가와 개념

1권에서 트라시마코스는 정의란 강한 자의 이익일 뿐이라고 주장한다(338c). 2권에서 플라톤의 형 글라우콘과 아데이만토스는 한 걸음 더 나아간다. 글라우콘은 쓰면 모습이 보이지 않게 되는 기게스의 반지 이야기(359a~360d)를 꺼내며, 들킬 염려가 없어도 사람이 정의로울 이유가 있는지 묻는다. 정의가 보상이나 평판 때문이 아니라 그 자체로 좋다는 것을 보여 달라는 요구다.

소크라테스는 개인의 정의를 곧바로 정의하지 않고 우회한다. 글씨가 작아 읽기 어려우니 같은 글을 더 큰 글씨로 쓴 곳, 곧 도시에서 먼저 정의를 찾자는 것이다(368c~369a). 그는 사람마다 잘하는 일이 다르므로 한 사람이 한 가지 일을 맡는 분업에서 도시가 생긴다고 설명한다(369b 이하). 도시가 커져 방어가 필요해지면서 통치자, 보조자(군인), 생산자의 세 계층이 나온다. 그 도시에는 지혜, 용기, 절제, 정의라는 네 덕이 있다. 정의는 각 계층이 자기 일을 하고 남의 일에 끼어들지 않는 것이다(433a~b).

이 틀은 사람의 마음으로 되돌아온다. 소크라테스는 한 사람 안에서도 서로 어긋나는 욕구가 생긴다는 점에서, 영혼에 이성, 기개(thymos), 욕구의 세 부분이 있다고 논증한다(435e~441c). 시체를 보고 싶은 마음과 그런 자신을 꾸짖는 마음이 한 사람 안에서 다투는 레온티오스 이야기가 그 예다(439e~440a). 정의로운 사람은 이성이 다스리고 나머지가 따르면서 세 부분이 각자의 일을 하는 사람이다(443c~444a). 도시의 정의와 영혼의 정의가 같은 모양이라는 것이 이 책의 구조다.

구체적 텍스트나 사례

이 이상 도시는 지금 기준으로 낯설다. 통치 계층은 사유재산을 갖지 않고 공동 숙소에서 함께 먹고 산다(416d~417b). 5권에서는 가족까지 이들 사이에서 공유하자고 한다. 시민들이 자기 자리를 받아들이게 하려고 사람이 금, 은, 청동의 혼으로 태어난다는 ‘고귀한 거짓말(noble lie)’도 제안한다(414b~415d). 다스리는 자는 철인왕(philosopher-king)이다. 소크라테스는 철학자가 왕이 되거나 왕이 철학을 하게 되기 전에는 나라의 불행이 그치지 않을 것이라고 말한다(473c~d). 8권에서는 현실의 정체가 명예정, 과두정, 민주정, 참주정으로 무너져 내리는 모습을 같은 이름의 영혼 유형과 짝지어 그린다.

철학적 의미

이 이상 도시를 어떻게 읽을지는 갈린다. 칼 포퍼는 『열린 사회와 그 적들』(1945)에서 이를 전체주의의 청사진이라고 비판했다. 반대로 이 도시는 현실에서 짓자는 설계도가 아니라 영혼을 비춰 보이기 위한 모형이라는 해석도 있다. 아리스토텔레스는 이미 『정치학』 2권에서 재산과 가족의 공유가 오히려 돌봄을 약하게 만든다고 반박했다. 확실한 것은 플라톤에게 정의가 규칙의 목록이 아니라 부분들이 제자리를 찾은 상태, 곧 질서의 문제라는 점이다.

동아시아 · 맹자: 차마 하지 못하는 마음에서 나오는 정치

시대적 배경

맹자(孟子)는 기원전 4세기에 살았다. 동주의 왕은 이름뿐인 존재가 되었고 제후국들이 서로 전쟁을 벌이던 시기다. 생몰 연대는 전통적으로 기원전 372년경~289년경이라고 하지만 학계에서 확정된 값은 아니다. 사마천의 『사기』는 그가 공자의 손자 자사의 문인에게 배웠다고 전한다. 『맹자』에는 그가 양(梁)의 혜왕과 제(齊)의 선왕 같은 군주를 찾아가 나눈 문답이 실려 있다. 군주들은 부국강병을 원했고, 맹자는 그 요구를 정면으로 되받았다.

핵심 개념

『맹자』 1A1은 이렇게 시작한다. 혜왕이 “내 나라를 어떻게 이롭게 하겠느냐”고 묻자 맹자는 “왕께서는 어찌 이익을 말씀하십니까? 인의(仁義)가 있을 뿐입니다”라고 답한다. 윗사람이 이익을 앞세우면 아랫사람도 서로 빼앗으려 한다는 것이다. 이 답이 맹자 정치론의 출발점이다.

이 답을 받치는 것이 성선(性善)이다. 맹자는 고자(告子)와 논쟁하면서(6A1~2) 사람의 본성이 선한 것은 물이 아래로 흐르는 것과 같다고 말한다. 가장 유명한 논증은 2A6의 우물가 아이다. 어린아이가 막 우물에 빠지려는 것을 보면 누구나 깜짝 놀라고 안쓰러워한다. 아이의 부모와 사귀려는 속셈이나 칭찬을 얻으려는 마음, 비난이 싫어서가 아니다. 맹자는 여기서 측은(惻隱), 수오(羞惡), 사양(辭讓), 시비(是非)의 마음을 인의예지의 ‘싹(端)’이라고 부른다. 싹이 곧 완성된 덕은 아니다. 사람은 이것을 키우고 넓혀 가야 한다(확충, 擴充, extension). SEP는 ‘근본적으로 선하다’는 표현이 맹자 본인이 아니라 후대 주희의 풀이에서 온다고 짚는다.

구체적 텍스트나 사례

2A6은 이 마음이 정치로 이어지는 길도 보여 준다. 남의 고통을 차마 보지 못하는 마음(不忍人之心)으로 정치를 하면 그것이 곧 인정(仁政)이다. 그렇다면 인정은 구체적으로 무엇인가. 맹자는 3A3에서 일정한 생업(恒産)이 있어야 일정한 마음(恒心)이 있다고 말하고, 우물 정(井) 자 모양으로 밭을 아홉 구역으로 나누는 정전제를 이상으로 든다. 여덟 가구가 각자 한 구역을 경작하고 가운데 한 구역은 함께 일군다. 이 제도가 실제로 시행되었는지는 불확실하며, 맹자가 이상화한 주나라 제도로 읽는 해석이 있다. 2A3에서 그는 힘으로 인을 빌려 쓰는 패도(霸道)와 덕으로 인을 행하는 왕도(王道)를 가른다.

철학적 의미

군주의 자리도 조건부다. 1B8에서 맹자는 폭군 주(紂)를 죽인 것은 임금을 시해한 것이 아니라 한낱 사내를 처단한 것이라고 말한다. 7B14의 “백성이 가장 귀하고, 사직이 그다음이며, 임금이 가장 가볍다”는 구절과 함께 읽으면, 통치의 정당성이 백성의 삶을 돌보는 데서 나온다는 주장이다. 이를 오늘의 민주주의와 곧바로 같다고 읽는 것은 조심해야 한다. 맹자는 백성이 통치자를 뽑는다고 말하지 않았다.

통합적 사유
SYMPOSIUM두 텍스트는 같은 날 놓고 읽을 만한 닮은 구조를 갖고 있다. 둘 다 한 사람의 안쪽과 한 나라의 질서를 하나의 틀로 묻는다. 플라톤은 도시에서 영혼으로, 맹자는 우물가의 한 사람에서 정치로 나아간다. 그러나 질서의 근거는 다르다. 플라톤에게 질서는 부분들이 기능에 따라 나뉘고 앎을 가진 소수가 이끌 때 서고, 통치자는 재산을 포기한다. 맹자에게 질서의 바탕은 누구에게나 있는 마음의 싹이고, 통치의 일은 백성에게 일정한 생업을 보장해 그 싹이 자랄 조건을 만드는 것이다. 맹자는 6B2에서 누구나 요순이 될 수 있다고 말한다. 강의자의 생각으로는 두 입장의 차이를 ‘누가 다스리는가’보다 ‘다스림이 무엇을 길러 내는가’의 차이로 읽을 때 비교가 풍부해진다. 이것은 해석이지 두 사상가가 말한 문장은 아니다.

Who is the “we” of the commons? Three units, one word, three answers

Three units use “commons” as a working term, and each one answers the same hidden question differently: who counts as a member, and what keeps the shared thing shared? The comparison is a way to read the theory underneath, so that a portfolio can say which answer it is giving.

Unit descriptions: the editors’ reading of the AA, RCA and Bartlett booklets, listed under the Bartlett PG24, PG16 and RCA ADS3 codes in curriculum/units.json

The lineage

1 · A word that arrives with an accusation

For most architecture students, “commons” first arrives as a warning. Garrett Hardin’s “The Tragedy of the Commons” appeared in Science in 1968, and its herdsmen sharing a pasture became the textbook picture of shared resources collapsing. The underlying logic had been sketched by William Forster Lloyd in 1833. Hardin’s own remedies pointed to private ownership or to state control, and the word spent years attached to the idea that what is held in common will be used up.

2 · Ostrom’s counter-case

Elinor Ostrom’s Governing the Commons (Cambridge University Press, 1990) argued that the dogma had hardened too quickly. Her cases were Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese forests and Spanish irrigation systems that communities had managed sustainably under rules of their own making. From them she drew a set of eight design principles, among them clearly defined boundaries, monitoring and graduated sanctions, and she shared the 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. For an architect the useful point is that a commons is not a condition of nature. It is an arrangement with a boundary, a rule and someone watching, and every one of those can be drawn.

3 · The historical and spatial turns

Two later bodies of work moved the term from economics into history and space. Peter Linebaugh’s The Magna Carta Manifesto (University of California Press, 2008) recovers the Charter of the Forest, issued alongside Magna Carta to protect the subsistence rights of ordinary people, and reads enclosure as the loss of those rights. Stavros Stavrides, an architect at the National Technical University of Athens, wrote Common Space: The City as Commons (Zed Books, 2016). He asks for space that is governed by all and open to all, beyond the split between public and private, and his opening chapter is titled “An urban archipelago of enclosures”. A third strand, hydrosocial territory, was introduced by Boelens and colleagues in Water International in 2016. It treats a water system as a configuration of people, institutions, flows and technology organised around the control of water.

4 · Three answers to “who is we?”

The editors read the unit descriptions as follows; this is our reading, not a statement of the tutors’ positions. Bartlett PG24 pairs commons with periphery and centre, self-governance, diaspora and walking as a method. Its “we” is a community of belonging, and the question is who is let in or pushed out when people move. Bartlett PG16 sets water security beside hydrosocial territory, enclosure and commons. Its “we” is a watershed, and Ostrom’s first principle, a clear boundary, is hard to draw when water crosses every line on the map. RCA ADS3 works with companion species, disturbed land and transhumance. Its “we” includes non-human users, so the rule that keeps a pasture shared has to be written for herds, soil and seasons as well as for people. Read side by side, the three units show that the answer is a design decision: a community, a watershed and a multispecies landscape each draw a different line around the commons.

5 · What the portfolio has to prove

Ostrom’s principles double as a drawing test. A plan shows the boundary and who may cross it. A section shows what is maintained, by whom and how often. A calendar or timeline shows the rule: when the pasture opens, when the sluice closes, when a user is sanctioned. A map of enclosures shows who was left outside, the Stavrides question. A commons drawn only as a pleasant shared room has skipped the rule, and a reviewer at any of these schools will ask where it is.

6 · The tension to name

Ostrom and Stavrides pull in different directions, and a strong portfolio says which way it leans. Ostrom’s first principle needs a boundary so that users can be told apart from outsiders. Stavrides wants space that is open to all and distrusts the closed enclave, even a well-governed one. Neither is simply right. A fenced pasture with fair rules can still exclude the people who most need it, and a space open to everyone can still be consumed by whoever arrives first. Naming where your project sits between those poles, and what it gives up by sitting there, is worth more than another shared kitchen.
