Front page Profession · Regulation · Education · UK

A word, not a competence: after a year off the register and “the threat of prosecution hanging over me”, the RIBA president rejoins the ARB — and says the experience proves the system polices a title rather than the work

The Architects’ Journal (19 November) reports that Chris Williamson has reversed last year’s decision not to renew his membership of the Architects Registration Board. In an opinion piece written for the AJ the same day, he insists that this is “not a step backwards or a change in my views”: a year on both sides of the register has, he argues, only exposed “the absurdity of the system as it stands today”.

Williamson let his membership lapse at the end of 2025, arguing that protecting only the title ‘architect’, rather than specific functions, left the competence of others providing architectural services unchecked. The RIBA called at the same time for certain activities, including submitting full planning applications, to be reserved for registered professionals. In his letter to members he writes that he spent the year fearing “a court case that could lead to travel bans, fines and substantial legal costs”, simply for using the title in meetings and conferences, while remaining free to provide every service an architect provides. In the AJ he puts it more bluntly: “When a regulator only focuses on policing the use of one word, it cannot provide sufficient assurance about the competence of everyone making critical decisions affecting our homes and buildings.”

The campaign does not change. The RIBA wants the Architects Act repealed, which would effectively abolish the ARB, new legislation defining reserved activities in the planning process, and a Built Environment Council to oversee competence across the construction industry; a forthcoming RIBA white paper will set out who is responsible for safety-critical functions. The AJ notes that his protest did not prompt a wave of resignations: in January it reported that numbers on the register had stayed broadly the same. For a Diploma student, the question is not abstract. The title is what the Part 3 at the end of the route confers; Williamson’s year asks what, exactly, it protects, and for whom. Neither AJ piece carries a response from the ARB. Single publication (two AJ pieces).

AI · Technology · Infrastructure

Datacentres · Energy · Carbon (19 Nov)

The cloud has a chimney: the IEA’s annual outlook finds AI’s growth being powered by gas in the US and coal in China faster than by the renewables its developers buy

The Guardian (19 November) reports on the International Energy Agency’s annual outlook, which expects datacentres to consume twice as much electricity by 2030 as today, with AI accounting for three-quarters of that growth, and their associated emissions to rise by 70 per cent. Around half the electricity for China’s AI datacentres would come from coal by the end of the decade; in the US, gas would supply about 60 per cent, with plans in August for about 180GW of new gas plants linked to datacentres, almost as much as the EU’s total installed gas capacity. In the EU, by contrast, the agency expects renewables to supply about 70 per cent.

The method matters as much as the forecast. For what the Guardian calls a “notoriously opaque industry”, the IEA worked from annual shipments of AI servers and used space-based imagery to track the construction of 93 large “AI factories”. It puts the world’s existing datacentre stock at 120GW, 80 per cent of it in China, the US and the EU, with a further 120GW expected by 2030. Datacentre developers are already the world’s biggest corporate buyers of renewable energy; the report suggests that is not enough. Single source (the Guardian, reporting the IEA).

CRITThe IEA had to watch these buildings from orbit to count them. If a building type can only be measured from space, who is it designed for? Draw one datacentre as a section through its supply: from the server hall back to the gas turbine or the coal seam.

City of London · Heritage · Demolition (19 Nov)

An Edwardian gateway comes down so that a Rogers-era office can be seen: RSHP wins permission to demolish 68 Fenchurch Street over the objections of Historic England and the Victorian Society

The Architects’ Journal (19 November) reports that the City of London approved, under delegated powers on 12 November, the demolition of 68 Fenchurch Street, a 1910 building by Paul Hoffman that stands at the entrance to RSHP’s Lloyd’s Register office at 70 Fenchurch Street. In its place RSHP proposes a double-height, partly elevated events building for the developer YardNine, “a modern interpretation of a traditional City banqueting house”, which would make the set-back office easier to see and reach from the street. The developer says the building, empty since 2024, cannot meet modern fire and accessibility standards.

The Victorian Society argued that there was no structural reason for demolition and that the building’s condition reflected “a lack of use and upkeep, rather than a critical structural flaw”; Historic England called it a “positive contributor” to the Lloyd’s Avenue Conservation Area. Planning officers replied that 1990s alterations had already cost it its “free-style eccentricism”. In a separate consent the steelwork of No 70 will change from cobalt blue to anthracite grey, which the Twentieth Century Society questioned. Single source (the AJ).

CRITThe demolished building’s offence is that it hides a better-known one. Is visibility a good enough reason to remove a building in a conservation area? Draw the street elevation twice, with and without No 68, and mark what each version lets a passer-by understand.

Edinburgh · Practice · Procurement (19 Nov)

The practice that designed the first Maggie’s Centre closes: Richard Murphy Architects winds up after 35 years, blaming “box-ticking” public procurement and the absence of competitions

The Architects’ Journal (19 November) reports that Richard Murphy Architects, founded in Edinburgh in 1991, filed a resolution to wind up with Companies House on 18 November. Murphy said “the absence of competitions and the box-ticking system of public procurement” had disadvantaged a practice of its size until it “dwindled to nine staff and three projects”; when the largest contract was terminated in August, the cost of redundancies made continuing impossible. The practice designed the first Maggie’s Centre in 1996.

Tamsie Thomson, chief executive of the RIAS, named “sclerotic procurement and planning systems, a fragmented construction industry and a grim economic outlook”, and said further insolvencies “should not be the price paid for government to take notice”. The AJ places the closure after Adam Khan Architects’ in October and Levitt Bernstein’s rescue plan last week. Earlier this year the practice was replaced by Reiach and Hall on the Thomas Hamilton building on Calton Hill; the client trust says it reviewed its partnerships for the project’s long-term delivery. Single source (the AJ).

CRITCompetitions reward an idea; frameworks reward a record. Which of the two lets a small practice exist? Redraw a public commission as a sequence of forms to be filled in, and mark the first box a practice of nine people cannot tick.

Representation · Evidence · Syria, Cyprus, Barcelona (18 Nov)

A plan drawn from footsteps and echoes: an ArchDaily essay reads Saydnaya, Famagusta and Barcelona as cases where architecture must be reconstructed from memory rather than measurement

ArchDaily (18 November) sets three projects side by side: the reconstruction of Saydnaya Prison in Syria, Hands-on Famagusta in Cyprus, and Memoria Cronotópica in Barcelona, places that are respectively inaccessible, divided by conflict, or gone. The longest case is Forensic Architecture’s 2016 work with Amnesty International, which rebuilt the prison’s interior from the memories of five former detainees interviewed in Istanbul, starting from satellite imagery and correcting the model as witnesses moved through it at eye level.

Measurements came from bodies: one survivor remembered the size of a cell-door hatch because guards had forced his head through it; another counted floor tiles. With the audio investigator Lawrence Abu Hamdan, the team gathered what it called “ear-witness testimony”, using simulated echoes to estimate rooms that detainees had rarely been allowed to see. Where accounts disagreed, discrepancies were documented rather than smoothed over. Single source (ArchDaily).

CRITA measured drawing claims certainty; a testimony carries doubt. How would you draw a plan that keeps its uncertainties visible? Draw a room you know only from memory, then mark every line by how sure you are of it.

서양 · 엘리스의 피론

시대적 배경

알렉산드로스가 기원전 323년에 죽은 뒤 그리스 세계는 거대한 왕국들로 갈라졌고, 폴리스의 시민이 공동의 일을 함께 판단하던 시대는 저물었다. 철학의 질문도 “좋은 국가는 무엇인가”에서 “흔들리는 세계에서 개인은 어떻게 평온할 수 있는가”로 옮겨 갔다. 에피쿠로스학파와 스토아학파는 각각 세계에 대한 확실한 설명(원자, 로고스)을 먼저 세우고 그 위에 평온을 쌓으려 했다. 피론(Pyrrho, 기원전 365/360년경~275/270년경)은 같은 목표를 정반대의 길로 찾은 사람으로 전한다.

그는 펠로폰네소스의 엘리스 출신이고, 파우사니아스는 엘리스의 시장에 그의 조각상이 있었다고 적었다. 아낙사르코스와 함께 알렉산드로스의 원정을 따라 인도까지 갔고 그곳에서 ‘벌거벗은 현자들’(gymnosophistai)을 만났다고 전한다. 디오게네스 라에르티오스는 이 만남이 그의 철학을 낳았다고 말하지만, 그 근거가 무엇인지는 분명하지 않다는 것이 학계의 대체적 평가다. 영향 관계로 단정할 수 없는 대목이다.

핵심 사상가와 개념

피론은 아무것도 쓰지 않았다. 그래서 그의 생각은 제자 티몬의 보고에 기대어 재구성된다. 가장 중요한 증거는 소요학파의 아리스토클레스가 티몬을 요약한 대목인데, 이 글은 다시 기독교 저술가 에우세비오스의 인용으로만 남았다. 세 겹의 전달을 거친 셈이다. 그 대목은 세 가지를 묻는다. 사물은 본성상 어떠한가, 우리는 사물에 어떤 태도를 가져야 하는가, 그렇게 하면 무엇이 따르는가.

첫째 답은 사물이 adiaphora(구별되지 않는), astathmēta(잴 수 없는), anepikrita(판정할 수 없는) 것이라는 말이다. 그러므로 감각도 의견도 참을 말하지도 거짓을 말하지도 않는다. 둘째 답은 의견 없이, 어느 쪽으로도 기울지 않고, 흔들리지 않는 태도를 가지라는 것이다. 각 사물에 대해 “이다”보다 더 “이다”인 것도 아니고 “아니다”보다 더 “아니다”인 것도 아니라고 말하라는 정식이 함께 전한다. 셋째로 그 결과는 먼저 말하지 않음(aphasia), 그다음 마음의 동요 없음(ataraxia)이다.

텍스트와 해석

이 짧은 글은 두 갈래로 읽힌다. 하나는 형이상학적 해석이다. 사물 자체가 본래 확정되어 있지 않다는 주장이라면, 피론은 엄밀한 의미의 회의주의자라기보다 “세계는 무규정적이다”라는 독특한 형이상학자가 된다. 다른 하나는 인식론적 해석이다. 우리가 사물의 본성을 판정할 수 없다는 주장이라면, 피론은 후대 회의주의의 선구자가 된다. 사본의 그리스어 자체가 두 읽기를 모두 허락하고, 문장의 상당 부분이 피론이 아니라 티몬의 것일 수 있다는 지적도 있다.

피론의 이름은 기원전 1세기 아이네시데모스가 되살린 ‘피론주의’ 전통에 붙었고, 2세기 무렵의 섹스투스 엠피리쿠스에 이르러 체계를 갖춘다. 판단 중지(epochē, suspension of judgement)를 중심어로 삼아, 대립하는 주장들이 같은 무게를 가질 때 판단을 멈추고 그 뒤에 평온이 따라온다고 설명한 것은 이 후대 전통이다. 강의자의 생각으로는, 피론 자신을 섹스투스의 정교한 방법론으로 거꾸로 읽지 않도록 조심해야 한다. 피론에게 남은 것은 방법이라기보다 삶의 태도에 가까운 몇 줄이다.

동아시아 · 조나라의 공손룡

시대적 배경

전국시대 후반, 제후들은 서로 경쟁하며 말 잘하는 인재를 식객으로 불러 모았다. 외교와 전쟁의 명분을 다투는 자리에서 말은 곧 힘이었다. 공손룡(公孫龍, 기원전 320년경~250년경)은 북방 조(趙)나라의 평원군(平原君, 기원전 252년 사망)을 섬겼고, 조나라 혜문왕에게 전쟁을 말리는 조언을 했다고 전한다. 『여씨춘추』와 『장자』에 그에 관한 일화가 실려 있다.

그를 묶는 ‘명가(名家)’라는 이름은 한(漢)대 서지학자들이 등석, 윤문, 혜시, 공손룡 등 일곱 사람을 한데 분류하면서 생겼다. 스스로 하나의 학파를 이룬 것이 아니라 후대가 만든 분류라는 해석이 지금은 널리 받아들여진다. 이들의 저술은 거의 남지 않았고, 우리는 대부분 다른 책의 인용과 일화로 그들을 안다.

핵심 사상가와 개념

『공손룡자』의 「백마론(白馬論)」은 묻고 답하는 대화다. 논자는 상식적인 반론자에 맞서 “흰 말은 말이 아니다”를 지킨다. 첫 논증은 이렇다. “馬者，所以命形也；白者，所以命色也。命色者非命形也。故曰：白馬非馬。” ‘말’은 모양을 이름 짓는 말이고 ‘희다’는 색을 이름 짓는 말이니, 색을 이름 짓는 것은 모양을 이름 짓는 것이 아니라는 것이다. 둘째 논증은 더 일상적이다. “求馬，黃、黑馬皆可致；求白馬，黃、黑馬不可致。” 말을 구하면 누런 말도 검은 말도 데려올 수 있지만, 흰 말을 구하면 그럴 수 없다. 그러니 ‘흰 말’과 ‘말’은 같은 것이 아니다.

텍스트와 해석

이 논증들은 오래 ‘궤변’으로 불렸다. 스탠퍼드 철학백과의 정리를 따르면, 논자는 “A는 B이다”를 술어 관계(흰 말은 말의 일종이다)가 아니라 동일성 관계(흰 말은 말과 같다)로 읽으며 그 사이를 오간다(equivocation). 해석은 갈린다. 20세기 초에는 이 대화를 보편자(universals)에 관한 논의로 읽었지만, 고대 중국에 보편자 실재론이 없었다는 이유로 지금은 이 읽기에 반대하는 쪽이 많다. 채드 핸슨과 A. C. 그레이엄은 부분과 전체의 관계로, 핸슨은 나중에 ‘흰 말’ 같은 복합어가 구성어의 지시 범위를 바꾼다는 문제로 읽었다. 그레이엄은 첫 논증을 “논리적 비약”이라 부르기도 했다.

현존 『공손룡자』는 짧은 대화 다섯 편과 서문으로 되어 있는데, 서문은 분명히 후대의 것이고, 그레이엄은 「백마론」과 「지물론(指物論)」 등 일부만 선진 시대 원문일 가능성이 높다고 보았다. 일본 학계에는 ‘백마비마’ 명제가 공손룡의 독창이라기보다 당시 변자(辯者)들 사이에 퍼져 있던 명제였다는 해석도 있다. 평원군의 집에서 공손룡이 “아무개에게는 귀가 셋이 있다”고 논증하자 공천(孔穿)이 “말하기는 매우 어렵지만 실제로는 틀렸다”고 받아쳤다는 일화는, 당대 사람들도 이 논법을 현실과 맞지 않는 재주로 보았음을 보여 준다.

Bartlett PG11 Access to Tools

Access to tools: what a mail-order catalogue from 1968 asks of an architecture unit in 2026

PG11 takes the Whole Earth Catalog (1968–72) as both provocation and precedent: an introductory project built on its logic, then buildings that treat architecture itself as a tool for planetary-scale problems. The interesting question is not whether the catalogue was right, but which of its promises survived and which were quietly converted into something else.

Tutors: Laura Allen, Mark Smout

The lineage

1 · The brief, in summary

The Bartlett’s own unit page could not be opened from this desk, so there is no verbatim quotation today. In summary, from the published 2025–26 brief: PG11 starts from the catalogue’s subtitle, “access to tools”, and asks what architecture becomes when it is designed as an instrument rather than an object, something that lets people act on systems far larger than a site. The catalogue structures the first project and then feeds the building designs that follow; the unit page also lists a field trip to Turin and Milan. The unit’s concept list reads as a scale ladder: object, system, environment.

2 · What the catalogue actually said

Stewart Brand’s first Whole Earth Catalog appeared in autumn 1968. Its opening page set out a purpose and a function. The purpose begins, famously, “We are as gods and might as well get used to it,” and goes on to describe “a realm of intimate, personal power” in which individuals “conduct [their] own education, find [their] own inspiration, shape [their] own environment”. The function is more modest and, for architects, more useful: the catalogue “functions as an evaluation and access device”. An item was listed if it was useful as a tool, relevant to independent education, high quality or low cost, not already common knowledge, and easily available by mail.

Read those five criteria as a design brief and the catalogue stops looking like a period piece. It is a curatorial machine: it does not make things, it selects, reviews and routes. The first section, “Understanding Whole Systems”, opened with Buckminster Fuller and set titles on general systems, cybernetics and Christopher Alexander’s Notes on the Synthesis of Form beside photographs of the whole Earth. The “Shelter and Land Use” section put dome manuals, tipis and tensile structures next to Architectural Design. Architecture appeared in it not as a discipline with authority but as one more set of tools a reader might pick up.

3 · Three readings of “tool”

The concept has a contested lineage, and the unit is strongest when it keeps the dispute open. The first reading is the Californian one that Fred Turner traced in From Counterculture to Cyberculture (2006): the Whole Earth network carried a countercultural faith in small tools and systems thinking into the culture of personal computing and, later, into digital utopianism. On this reading, “access to tools” is the ancestor of the platform, and its politics drift towards the individual consumer.

The second reading is Ivan Illich’s. Tools for Conviviality (1973) called a society “convivial” when modern tools serve politically interrelated individuals rather than managers, and named the opposite a “radical monopoly”: when one tool, such as the car, becomes so dominant that it makes the alternatives impossible. Illich gives the architect a test the catalogue never stated: a tool is not good because it is available, but because it widens what people can do together without making them dependent on a single system.

The third reading came from Milan. In January 1973, in the offices of Casabella, Ettore Sottsass, Alessandro Mendini, Andrea Branzi and groups including Archizoom, Superstudio, 9999, UFO and Zziggurat set up Global Tools, which its founders described as a counter-school of manual work and design. Branzi’s writing of that year answered Illich’s Deschooling Society. Here “tools” meant pedagogy: making things by hand to unlearn the design profession. That is presumably not incidental to a unit travelling to Milan.

4 · The critique the portfolio has to absorb

Felicity D. Scott’s Outlaw Territories (2016) is the necessary counterweight. Scott follows architecture and the environment through the 1960s and 1970s into the machinery of security, displacement and counterinsurgency, setting countercultural settlement movements against the expanded reach of the state. Her book makes it hard to treat self-built domes and planetary photographs as innocent. The same systems vocabulary that promised personal power also served the institutions the catalogue claimed to bypass.

For a PG11 portfolio, this means the drawing set has to show who holds the tool, who maintains it, and what it makes impossible. A building presented as a “device” is persuasive only when its operating sequence is drawn: the manual, the parts list, the order of assembly, the second user who adapts it. The catalogue’s own format suggests a method. A spread of reviewed tools, each with its source and its price, is already an architectural drawing type. The risk is the opposite: a beautiful kit of parts with no politics, the 1968 optimism without the 1973 doubt.
