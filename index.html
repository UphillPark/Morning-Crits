Front page Reuse · Legacy · Public space · Hackney Wick · London

Built from waste, paid for by the flats around it: Hackney Wick’s new Yard theatre is held up against the Olympic legacy’s official culture quarter

In the Guardian (27 October), Oliver Wainwright reviews the Yard’s new home beside the Olympic park, a low-budget theatre by Takero Shimazaki that reuses a crane tower, unfired bricks and a community levy. His verdict is that it “feels truly grown from its place”, and he sets it against East Bank in Stratford, which he finds “airlifted in from on high”.

According to the review, the Yard began in 2011 as a shoestring warehouse theatre fitted out by the young Practice Architecture, a bricolage of butcher’s curtains, pink plasterboard and scavenged scaffolding planks that was meant to last a few months and lasted more than a decade. Its founder, Jay Miller, pitched the owner of the vacant building on his occupying it to save security costs. The new building, on the same site, cost £4m, of which £3m came from the community infrastructure levy (CIL) collected by the London Legacy Development Corporation; as Wainwright puts it, “the apartment blocks rising all around the Yard helped to pay for its existence”. The old crane tower is now a polycarbonate-sheathed ventilation shaft that glows at night, the brick wall behind the stage was salvaged on site, and the bricks that spell out the theatre’s name are unfired K-Briqs, which the review says are 95% construction waste.

The argument is as much about style as about carbon. Shimazaki says he borrowed the symmetrical pitched roof and central tower of Palladian churches to give the building permanence, “as if the building might have been here far longer than it has”, while the seating that looks like cast concrete is cement particle board; he describes the project as an interest in “permanence in theatre, and fakery in architecture”. Wainwright contrasts all this with East Bank, the £1.1bn cultural quarter that houses branches of the V&A and Sadler’s Wells, UAL and UCL, which he calls “designed by committee” and “less than world class”. This is one critic’s judgement and the review quotes only the Yard’s side; this paper did not find a response from East Bank’s institutions or the LLDC, and has not visited either site.

CRIT · Question of the day

If a levy on the flats around it can pay for a theatre that looks nothing like them, is that planning gain or a quiet subsidy from development to its own alibi? And when a building deliberately fakes permanence, what should a crit mark it down for: the fake, or the honesty about it?

London · Skyline · Heritage (27 Oct)

A tower beside the Gherkin before it has an official height: WilkinsonEyre’s Exchequer Court scheme goes to consultation

According to the Architects’ Journal (27 October), the insurer AmTrust and the development manager Upper Brook have started a public consultation, running until 6 November, on a WilkinsonEyre tower to replace the seven-storey 1990s Exchequer Court at 33 St Mary Axe. It would provide more than 100,000 m² of offices, cultural space and 3,500 m² of publicly accessible space, with public uses on the first seven floors topped by a free double-height garden overlooking the Gherkin. The tower is described as vertically ribbed with a curved roofline; office floorplates are 1,950 to 2,100 m². The site lies beside the Grade I-listed St Helen’s Bishopsgate and inside the St Helen’s Place Conservation Area, and the AJ says the orientation and form are still being developed to respect views of the church.

The consultation boards do not state a height. The AJ notes that the Financial Times reported 300 m and 70 storeys, which would make it taller than the 225 m Cheesegrater and a little shorter than One London at 309.6 m, but it presents that as unconfirmed; the same FT account puts a planning application early next year and completion in 2033. The AJ article quotes no one and reports no objection from heritage bodies, so the sharper questions here are this paper’s own: a public is asked to respond to a form whose scale it has not been told.

CRITA consultation that withholds the one number a neighbour most needs is a drawing problem as much as a political one. Draw the tower’s silhouette from the churchyard at three possible heights, and decide which of them the church’s Grade I setting could survive.

Georgia · Tbilisi · Masterplan · Reuse (26 Oct)

Stefano Boeri Architetti wins the Loco Park masterplan: a Soviet locomotive factory becomes a brick-clad Tbilisi neighbourhood

Dezeen (26 October) reports that Stefano Boeri Architetti has won the competition to masterplan Loco Park, on the site of a Soviet-era locomotive factory in Tbilisi. The studio will renovate the existing factory and rebuild some of its original architectural features in a contemporary style, and add new buildings with homes and community spaces set in green space, with sports facilities, parks and boulevards. Apartments sit on the upper floors and shops, cafés and workshops at street level; roof forms recall vaulted, sawtooth and pitched industrial roofs, and brick cladding in blue-grey, terracotta, green, taupe and white is meant to reflect colours found in Tbilisi’s architecture.

Boeri says the neighbourhood will “combine the memory of the large Soviet-era locomotive factory” with new life, and his colleague Pietro Chiodi says colour and brick give each building “its own identity while bringing them together into a coherent whole”. Dezeen gives no site area, number of homes, client, cost or completion date and reports no criticism, so this paper cannot judge scale or who benefits. What can be read from the text is the method: memory is carried by rebuilt features and roof silhouettes rather than by retained fabric, which makes “reconstruction in a contemporary style” the phrase to interrogate.

CRITWhen an industrial site is remembered through its roof shapes and its colours, what exactly is being preserved: a building, a workforce or an image? List what stays, what is rebuilt and what is merely referenced, and say which of the three a former locomotive worker would recognise.

서양 · 플라톤 1: 그림자와 이데아

시대적 배경

플라톤은 기원전 428/427년경 아테네의 명문가에서 태어났다. 청년기에 아테네는 펠로폰네소스 전쟁에서 패했고(기원전 404년), 곧이어 스승 소크라테스가 시민 재판에서 사형을 선고받았다(기원전 399년). 전통적으로 기원전 387년경에 세운 것으로 전하는 학원 아카데메이아는 이 충격 이후 ‘올바른 삶과 올바른 도시는 무엇을 근거로 판단하는가’를 계속 묻는 자리였다. 플라톤의 저술은 거의 모두 대화편이며 소크라테스가 중심 화자로 나온다. 학계는 대화편을 초기·중기·후기로 나누는데, 오늘 다루는 이데아론은 『파이돈』과 『국가』 같은 중기 대화편에서 가장 두드러진다.

핵심 사상가와 개념

이데아(idea, eidos)는 여러 아름다운 것들이 ‘아름답다’고 불릴 수 있게 하는 ‘아름다움 자체’ 같은 것이다. 『파이돈』에서 형상은 ‘그 자체로’(auto kath’ auto) 있는 것, 곧 단순하고 변하지 않는 것으로 묘사되고, 감각되는 개별자들은 형상에 ‘관여’(분유, methexis, participation)함으로써 그것이 된다. 『국가』 6권의 ‘선분의 비유’(509d–511e)는 실재를 눈에 보이는 영역과 지성으로 이해되는 영역으로 나누고 각각을 다시 둘로 쪼갠다. 같은 책에서 ‘좋음의 이데아’는 다른 형상들이 이해될 수 있게 하는 근원이며, 존재 ‘너머’(epekeina tēs ousias, 509b)에 있다고까지 말해진다.

구체적 텍스트: 동굴의 비유

『국가』 7권(514a–517a)의 비유(allegory)에서 죄수들은 어릴 때부터 동굴 안에 묶여 앞쪽 벽만 본다. 뒤쪽에서는 불이 타고, 불과 죄수들 사이로 사람들이 물건을 들고 지나가며 그 그림자가 벽에 비친다. 죄수들은 그림자를 실재라고 믿는다. 한 사람이 풀려나 뒤돌아보면 불빛에 눈이 부시고(dazzled), 동굴 밖으로 끌려 올라가면 처음에는 그림자를, 다음에는 사물을, 마지막에 해를 보게 된다. 이 상승(ascent) 끝에 그가 돌아와 이야기하면 아직 묶여 있는 사람들은 그를 비웃고, 심지어 그를 죽이려 들 것이라고 소크라테스는 덧붙인다(517a). 소크라테스 자신이 해석하듯 동굴은 보이는 세계, 바깥은 이해되는 세계, 해는 좋음의 이데아에 대응한다(517b).

철학적 의미

사실과 해석을 나눠 적는다. 사실: 어느 대화편에도 ‘이데아론’을 한 번에 체계적으로 서술한 대목은 없다. 해석: 그래서 ‘이데아론’은 여러 대화편에서 학자들이 재구성한 것이며, 중기와 후기의 입장이 일관된가를 두고 학계가 갈린다(스탠퍼드 철학 백과사전의 정리). 『파르메니데스』에서는 플라톤 스스로 등장인물의 입을 빌려 형상과 개별자의 관계에 제기될 난점(이른바 ‘제3의 인간’ 논변)을 다룬다. 강의자의 생각으로는, 동굴의 비유가 오래 읽히는 이유는 형이상학 교리보다 ‘눈앞의 확실함이 그림자일 수 있다’는 경험을 한 장면으로 압축했기 때문이다.

동아시아 · 『도덕경』: 비어 있음의 쓰임

시대적 배경

『도덕경』은 전통적으로 노자(老子)가 썼다고 전하지만, 저자의 실존과 연대, 책이 한 번에 쓰였는가를 두고 학계의 이견이 크다. 1993년 후베이성 곽점(郭店)에서 나온 죽간 가운데 71매는 지금의 『도덕경』 81장 중 31개 장에 해당하는 내용을 담고 있고, 연대는 기원전 300년경까지 올라갈 수 있다고 본다. 이 죽간은 일부만 있고 통행본과 차이가 크다. 한(漢) 초의 무덤(마왕퇴, 馬王堆)에서 나온 비단 사본 두 종은 곽점본보다 통행본에 가깝다. 오늘 우리가 주로 읽는 통행본은 삼국 시대 왕필(王弼, 226–249)의 주석이 붙은 판본이다. 즉 ‘노자의 사상’이 아니라 여러 시기에 다른 모습으로 유통된 텍스트를 읽고 있다는 점을 먼저 기억해야 한다.

핵심 개념

11장의 첫 구절은 이렇다. “三十輻共一轂，當其無，有車之用.” 서른 개의 바퀴살이 한 바퀴통에 모이지만, 그 ‘없는 곳’(無)이 있어서 수레의 쓰임이 있다. 이어 진흙을 빚어 그릇을 만들 때도 그릇의 빈 곳이, 문과 창을 뚫어 방을 만들 때도 방의 빈 곳이 쓰임을 이룬다. 결론은 “故有之以為利，無之以為用”, 곧 있는 것은 이로움(利)을 주고 없는 것은 쓰임(用)을 만든다는 것이다. 여기서 無는 먼저 바퀴통의 구멍, 그릇의 속, 방의 빈 공간처럼 구체적인 ‘비어 있음’(hollow)이다. 통행본 48장의 “無為而無不為”로 요약되는 무위(無為)는 이를 행위의 방식으로 옮긴 개념으로 읽히지만, 판본마다 문구가 다르므로 한 줄로 못박지 않는다.

철학적 의미

해석이 갈리는 지점이 있다. 11장의 無를 ‘구체적인 빈 곳이 가진 쓰임’으로 읽을 수도 있고, 왕필처럼 無를 사물의 근본(本無)으로 끌어올려 읽을 수도 있다. 후자는 위진 시대의 해석이며 11장 자체가 말하는 것보다 많이 나아간 것이다. 같은 장의 대비는 ‘눈에 보이는 몸통’과 ‘그것을 쓸 수 있게 하는 빈 곳’이다. 이 빈 곳은 수레와 떨어진 다른 세계에 있지 않고 수레 한가운데에 있다. 강의자의 관찰로는, 동굴 밖의 해와 달리 11장의 無는 올라가서 보는 대상이 아니라 이미 쓰고 있으면서 보지 못하는 것에 가깝다.

자료: 곽점(郭店) 죽간, 마왕퇴(馬王堆) 비단 사본, 왕필본 『도덕경』 11장·48장.

통합적 사유
SYMPOSIUM두 텍스트는 모두 ‘눈앞에 보이는 것이 전부가 아니다’라고 말한다. 그러나 보이지 않는 것의 자리가 다르다. 플라톤에서 참된 것은 변하지 않는 형상 쪽에 있고, 개별 사물은 그것에 기대어 이해된다. 11장에서 쓰임을 만드는 것은 사물 한가운데의 빈 곳이다. 이를 ‘초월 대 내재’ 같은 한 쌍으로 서둘러 정리하면 도식이 되어 버린다. 플라톤의 형상도 사물들이 이해되게 하는 근거이고, 『도덕경』의 無도 후대에는 형이상학적 개념으로 읽혔기 때문이다. 오늘 얻을 것은 결론이 아니라 질문의 방향이다. 무엇이 ‘더 참된가’를 물을 때와 무엇이 ‘쓸 수 있게 하는가’를 물을 때, 우리는 같은 사물에서 서로 다른 것을 보게 된다.
핵심 질문 · 실습
  1. 동굴 안의 죄수에게 그림자가 ‘실재’인 것은 속았기 때문인가, 다른 것을 본 적이 없기 때문인가? 풀려난 사람이 돌아와 겪는 일(517a)은 이 둘 중 어느 쪽 답을 지지하는가?
  2. 11장은 ‘있는 것’(有)이 이로움을, ‘없는 것’(無)이 쓰임을 만든다고 말한다. 이로움과 쓰임을 구분하지 않으면 무엇을 놓치는가?
  3. 실습 (10분) · 늘 쓰는 물건 하나(컵, 의자, 방)를 고른다. ① 그것의 ‘이상적인 모습’을 한 문장으로 쓰고(플라톤식), ② 그것의 쓰임을 만드는 빈 곳을 한 문장으로 쓴다(11장식). 두 문장 중 어느 쪽이 그 물건을 더 잘 설명하는지, 한 문단으로 변론한다.

Three ways to name a turning point: why ADS0 puts Hitchcock, Cartier-Bresson and katastrophè in one sentence

ADS0 asks you to intervene in reality while it is still unfolding. Its brief reaches for three different vocabularies of the turning point, from film, photography and drama, and each assumes a different relation between the person who acts, the person who watches and the moment itself.

Tutors: Steve Salembier, Elin Eyborg Lund, Louis-Philippe Van Eeckhoutte

“…the moment of katastrophè, or, the overturning of the plot by a sudden change of events.”RCA ADS0 brief, 2025–26
The lineage

1 · What the brief is asking for

ADS0 describes itself as a studio with a focus on performance, scenography and installation, and it states its method plainly: staged intervention, performative activation and 1:1 design. The brief asks how “the unfolding spectacle of reality” can be shaped by staged interventions in space, and it is impatient with architecture that stays inside speculative simulation. The outputs it names are 1:1 spatial prototypes or staging devices that are performatively activated. Alongside the studio, the programme runs ten seminars on spatial practice within contemporary art, taught on Tuesdays in the first term.

Read quickly, this is a studio about building full-size things and putting people in them. Read slowly, it is a claim about time. The brief does not say that architecture should produce a better space. It says architecture should produce a turn: a point at which what was happening becomes something else. The three names it chooses all belong to that claim, and they are not interchangeable.

2 · Katastrophè: a turn built into a plot

The brief glosses katastrophè as the overturning of a plot by a sudden change of events, and treats it as the set of events or interventions that initiate radical change. Aristotle’s Poetics gives the nearest technical vocabulary, though not this word: his term for the turn is peripeteia, a change by which the action veers round to its opposite according to probability or necessity, and he rates it highest when it coincides with anagnorisis, the passage from ignorance to knowledge. The later habit of calling the closing phase of a plot its catastrophe, or of the unravelling that follows the turn its denouement, belongs to subsequent dramatic criticism rather than to Aristotle, and this note has not traced that history further.

What matters for a studio is the logic. A katastrophè is designed. The reversal follows from what came before, and an audience that sees it recognises it as necessary only in retrospect. That is a demanding standard for an architectural device: the intervention has to be built so that, when it is activated, the earlier situation reads differently.

3 · Cartier-Bresson: the turn found, not built

Henri Cartier-Bresson’s book appeared in 1952 in France as Images à la sauvette, roughly “images on the run”, published by Tériade’s Verve with a cover made for it by Henri Matisse. The American edition, issued the same year with Simon and Schuster, carried the title that stuck: The Decisive Moment. It reproduced 126 photographs. In the preface he describes photography, in essence, as the near-instant recognition of an event’s significance together with a precise organisation of forms.

This is the opposite temporal structure from katastrophè. The photographer does not build the turn; the photographer is present for it and selects it, and the decision is made by eye in a fraction of a second. For an architect that is unsettling, because buildings are slow and the decisive moment is not under their control. The brief’s answer is the staging device: if you cannot wait for the moment, you can prepare a place in which one is likely to occur, and you can make the form legible enough that it registers.

4 · Hitchcock: the turn held open

The brief’s third name is Hitchcock’s “moment of suspense”. Suspense differs from both of the others, because its force lies before the turn. The audience is positioned to anticipate something it cannot yet see, and the longer it waits, the more the space around the event matters: staircases, windows, doorways. Steven Jacobs’s study of Hitchcock’s architecture follows exactly this thread, from his early work as a set designer to the confined sets of Rope and Rear Window, and shows how motifs such as stairs and windows carry the narrative structure of suspense.

Put side by side, the three describe a sequence. Suspense is the held breath, the decisive moment is the instant of recognition, and katastrophè is the reversal that follows from both. A 1:1 intervention in an ongoing situation could be asked to do any of them, and a portfolio that does not say which is doing the work will be hard to read.

5 · The argument for the portfolio

Performance changes who the architect is accountable to. Claire Bishop’s Artificial Hells (2012) traces participatory and performance-based work from Futurism and Dada through the Situationist International to artists such as Thomas Hirschhorn and Tania Bruguera, and presses its claims about emancipation. For ADS0 the lesson is practical rather than historical: a staged intervention has an audience, and the audience either participates, is delegated to, or watches. You will need to say which, because the reading of the “turn” depends on it.

It is also a problem of evidence. A turning point happens once, in a place, to people who were there. The studio’s outputs, by contrast, are drawings, models, films and a prototype that others will assess later. So the portfolio has to prove the turn after the fact: a before and an after that a reviewer who was not present can compare, a record of the activation, and a drawing that shows what the device made possible that the site, on its own, did not. The assessable object is not the moment but the apparatus that produced it. That is the editor’s reading of the brief, not something it says in these words.
